A patent clerk who rewrote the universe

Einstein spent his days at the Swiss Patent Office in Bern, evaluating other people's inventions. He spent his evenings rethinking the nature of space, time, and light — with no laboratory, no university post, and no one watching.

What he produced from that quiet desk didn't just add to physics. It replaced the Newtonian picture of the universe that had stood for two centuries, and gave the twentieth century its new common sense: that time bends, that mass and energy are the same currency, and that the speed of light is the one constant everyone can agree on.

He would go on to hold posts in Zurich, Prague, Berlin, and finally Princeton — but the habit of mind never changed. He trusted a thought experiment as much as an equation, and treated curiosity as a discipline rather than a gift.

A life in motion
1879

Born in Ulm, Germany

A slow talker as a child, by his own account — the kind of person more interested in why the compass needle pointed north than in what other children were doing.

1905

The miracle year

While working full-time as a patent examiner, he published four papers that introduced special relativity, mass–energy equivalence, and the photoelectric effect, and confirmed the existence of atoms.

1915

General relativity

A decade of near-obsessive work produced a new theory of gravity — not a force, but a curve in the fabric of space and time itself.

1919

Confirmed by an eclipse

Starlight bending around the sun, observed during a solar eclipse, matched his prediction exactly. Overnight, he became the most famous scientist alive.

1933

Leaves Germany for good

With the Nazi rise to power, he renounced his German citizenship and settled permanently at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton.

1955

Dies in Princeton

He left behind an unfinished search for a unified theory — and a way of looking at the world that outlived the equations themselves.
